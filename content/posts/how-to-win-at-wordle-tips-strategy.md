Wordle gives you six guesses, but the real resource is information. Strong players do not merely hunt for a word that fits. They choose guesses that reveal useful letters, eliminate large groups of possibilities, and make every colored tile count.

There is no opener or tactic that guarantees a win every day. Still, a consistent strategy can reduce wasted guesses and make difficult boards much more manageable. ## Start with a strong opener

A useful first guess normally combines common vowels with common consonants and avoids repeated letters. Words such as these make sensible openers:

- `CRANE`
- `SLATE`
- `TRACE`
- `AUDIO`
- `SOARE`

These words do not all serve exactly the same purpose. `CRANE`, `SLATE`, and `TRACE` balance frequent vowels with productive consonants. `AUDIO` tests four vowels immediately, but it reveals less about common consonants. `SOARE` offers broad frequency coverage, although it is obscure and may not be accepted in every Wordle-style game.

Vowel coverage matters because it quickly exposes the likely sound structure of the answer. Consonant coverage matters just as much: letters such as `R`, `S`, `T`, `L`, and `N` help distinguish among many words that share the same vowels.

The practical rule is simple: begin with five different, frequently used letters. A repeated letter in your opener can be correct, but it usually tests less of the board.

## Use letter frequency to your advantage

In general English word lists, letters such as `E`, `S`, `A`, `O`, `R`, `I`, `L`, `T`, and `N` appear often. That makes them valuable early tests. Exact rankings vary with the word list, however, and Wordle answers are not a perfectly random sample of all English words.

Position matters too. A letter can be common overall without being equally likely in all five squares. `S`, for example, is especially useful at the beginning of many words, while `E` is productive in several positions and frequently appears near the end.

Do not treat frequency as a rigid formula. Use it to break ties. If two guesses fit the known clues, prefer the one that tests more common letters or puts a likely letter into a new, useful position.

## Avoid repeating gray letters

One of the most common beginner mistakes is reusing a letter that has already been ruled out. If `T` is gray and there are no duplicate-letter complications, a later guess containing `T` spends a valuable square confirming something you already know.

Before submitting a guess, scan it against the keyboard:

1. Does it contain a gray letter?
2. Does it place a yellow letter in a position already shown to be wrong?
3. Does it ignore a confirmed green position?
4. Does it test enough new information?

There is one important exception. When a guess contains the same letter twice, a gray copy does not always mean the letter is completely absent. If one `E` is green or yellow and the other `E` is gray, the answer usually contains exactly one `E`. Duplicate feedback is a counting clue, not just an absent/present clue.

## Use yellow letters smartly

A yellow tile tells you two things: the letter belongs in the answer, and it does not belong in the position you just tried. Both parts matter.

Move a yellow letter instead of leaving it in place. Better yet, choose a guess that tests it in a plausible new position while also introducing several untested letters. If `A` is yellow in the second square, a follow-up that moves `A` to the first, third, fourth, or fifth square can teach you more immediately.

When the same yellow letter survives across several guesses, keep track of every forbidden position. Four failed positions effectively force the fifth, even before the tile turns green.

## Lock in green letters

Green letters reveal the confirmed structure of the answer. Build around them and use the remaining spaces efficiently.

Suppose your pattern is `S _ A _ E`. Do not search mentally through every five-letter word. Search within that frame, then compare the letters that could occupy the two gaps. Thinking in patterns reduces cognitive load and makes word families easier to spot.

Outside Hard Mode, moving a green letter can occasionally be worthwhile when a probe word would separate several similar answers. Most of the time, however, keeping green tiles fixed prevents avoidable mistakes and focuses the next guess.

## Hard Mode strategy

Hard Mode requires each new guess to use the clues already revealed. That usually means keeping green letters in place and including known yellow letters.

It is harder because you cannot freely play an unrelated probe word. If the remaining candidates are `CATCH`, `HATCH`, `MATCH`, `PATCH`, and `WATCH`, a normal-mode player may test several distinguishing letters at once with a word that cannot be the answer. Hard Mode may force a slower choice from within the family.

Paradoxically, Hard Mode can also make decision-making easier. It enforces good habits: use confirmed letters, respect known positions, and stop submitting guesses that contradict the board.

To play Hard Mode well:

- Start with broad, nonrepeating letter coverage.
- Reposition yellow letters aggressively before the pattern becomes too restrictive.
- Anticipate word families and test their distinguishing letters early.
- Avoid locking into a narrow ending such as `_ATCH` until you have eliminated enough possible first letters.
- Treat duplicate letters carefully; Hard Mode rules may enforce known letters without fully explaining their count.

## Advanced tactics: maximize information

The best guess is not always the word most likely to be correct. Sometimes it is the word most likely to divide the remaining possibilities into smaller groups.

### Entropy-based guessing

In Wordle strategy, *entropy* is a way to measure how much information a guess is expected to reveal across the remaining answers. A high-information guess tends to produce varied feedback patterns, sharply reducing the candidate list regardless of which pattern appears.

You do not need to calculate entropy by hand. The useful principle is to test letters and positions that separate many remaining candidates, rather than choosing a guess that confirms the same feature repeatedly.

### Use probe words

A probe word is played mainly to test letters, even when it cannot be the answer. Imagine several candidates differ only by their first letter. Guessing one candidate tests one possibility; a carefully selected probe might test four possible starting letters at once.

Probe words are most useful in normal mode when:

- many candidates share the same pattern;
- you have enough guesses left;
- one information-rich word can eliminate several branches; and
- guessing candidates one at a time risks running out of turns.

### Know when to sacrifice a guess

Spending a turn on information can feel wasteful, but it may be the safest route to a win. If six plausible answers remain and only three guesses are left, blindly trying one candidate after another may fail. A probe that identifies the correct branch can turn the following guess into a near certainty.

Do not sacrifice a guess automatically. When only one or two candidates remain, attempt the likeliest valid answer. Use a probe when the expected information is worth more than the small chance of solving immediately.

## A practical turn-by-turn strategy

Use this compact routine during a game:

1. Open with five common, nonrepeating letters.
2. Remove gray letters from consideration, while checking for duplicate-letter clues.
3. Move every yellow letter to a new plausible position.
4. Keep green letters fixed unless normal mode gives you a compelling probe.
5. Use the second guess to cover new letters when the first guess reveals little.
6. Once the candidate set is small, switch from coverage to exact pattern solving.
7. If a large word family remains, use a probe before guesses run short.

This approach balances two goals: finding the answer and learning enough to find it on the next turn.
